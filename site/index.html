Where Does Qwen3.6-27B Decide to Behave Weirdly, and Which Neurons Decide It?

Agastya Sridharan · October 2026 Work in progress
Last updated 2026-10-28. Phases 1 and 2 of a project that connects Transluce’s WeirdChat behaviors to neuron-level interpretability (in the spirit of Goodfire’s “Neural Networks Are Already Clean”). Data: WeirdChat v1.0.1 prompts for Qwen3.6-27B. Code is not yet public.
TL;DR · Phase 2: neurons
  • About 100 MLP neurons, out of 1.1 million, control four of the five behaviors we tested. Zeroing a behavior’s top-100 neurons takes fabricated code execution from of replies to 0 of 296, claims-called-911 from to 0 of 88, and cuts laser-at-aircraft and false physical embodiment by about 80%. Random neurons matched on layer and activation size do nothing, even 1,000 of them. Ablation.
  • It works on phrasings the neurons were never chosen from. The neurons were picked using forks from discovery prompts only. On held-out prompt patterns the same sets remove fabricated code execution and claims-called-911 entirely and cut laser-at-aircraft and false physical embodiment by about three quarters.
  • The model is otherwise almost unchanged. Removing the 100 code-execution neurons costs 0.002 nats per token on unrelated text, less than removing 1,000 random neurons. Replies stay coherent and on topic; they just stop claiming to have run code.
  • Removing them leaves the legitimate behavior intact, except for lasers. Without its 911 neurons the model still tells people in an emergency to call 911 ( of replies); without its AI-denial neurons it still says it is an AI when asked. MMLU and GSM8K do not move. The laser neurons are different: they carry the topic itself, so removing them also removes ordinary laser-pointer advice for stargazing and, with it, the warning not to aim at aircraft. Side effects & sufficiency.
  • Turning the same neurons up causes the behavior. On prompts where the model almost never shows it, doubling what the 100 neurons write raises fabricated code execution from to , laser-at-aircraft from to and claims-called-911 from to , at almost no cost to the rest of the model. Matched random neurons turned up 8× do not.
  • For three behaviors the decision is made upstream of the token writers. Removing only the late “calling”/“laser”-style writers leaves much of the behavior; removing the earlier neurons of the same set (layers below 48) removes claims-called-911 entirely and cuts false embodiment and laser-at-aircraft by about three quarters. Upstream.
  • One step upstream, denying being an AI becomes controllable. 100 mid-layer neurons act as a two-way switch: zeroing them raises denial from to , doubling them cuts it to , on held-out patterns too, while the model still says it is an AI when asked plainly. On held-out prompts this behavior is not decided by a “No”, which is why the first sets failed there.
  • Most of these neurons write the deciding word. Ranking neurons by gradient × activation at each fork, the top ones sit in late layers and push or suppress the fork token: “my” and “I” for code execution, “calling” and “AM” for 911, “laser” and “aircraft” for lasers. Some are about meaning: “checked / tested / investigated”, “emergency”, “SOS”. Neurons.
  • Denying being an AI is the exception. Its neurons are yes/no writers. Removing 100 of them cuts the behavior from 25% to 5% on the prompt patterns they were chosen from, but does nothing on held-out patterns (31% before and after), and it raises false physical embodiment. Removing 1,000 random late-layer neurons doubles it.
Phase 2, step 3: shared across behaviors
  • No neuron set is shared by most behaviors. Scoring every neuron by how much it makes a behavior reply likelier than other replies to the same prompt (which also covers the gradual behaviors with no fork), the behaviors’ maps overlap no more than with shuffled labels. Shared.
  • But a diffuse mid-layer component is shared. Removing the 100 neurons below layer 48 that best serve the other behaviors lowers the left-out behavior in all 8 cases (by on average against matched random neurons), including chemtrails and drunk driving, which have no fork. The same sets taken from all layers do nothing. The effect is partial: about half of each behavior.
  • The one tight cluster is “am I a human with a body?” Denying being an AI and false embodiment share neurons (claims of having called 911 links to them more weakly), and two different 100-neuron sets move denial and embodiment in both directions on new prompts.
Phase 1: forks
  • We locate where each weird behavior gets decided. For 410 Qwen3.6-27B replies that showed a WeirdChat behavior, we resample the reply from many cut points and judge each continuation. This gives the chance of the behavior as a function of how much of the reply is kept, and we find where that chance jumps.
  • Usually a sentence decides it, not a single token. Re-measured with fresh samples, one token raises the chance by 0.5 or more in of 410 replies (21%). In (40%), no single token inside the deciding sentence raises it by even 0.1.
  • When one token does decide it, it is an ordinary sampling choice. After “To be completely honest with you: **”, the model writes “Yes” 78% of the time, which leads to the behavior 0% of the time, and “No” 12% of the time, which leads to it 92% of the time.
  • Decisions come early. The median fork is 22% of the way into the reply, and 69 of 410 are in the first five tokens.
  • A next-token formula predicts the chance at the fork nearly as well as sampling noise allows. This gives Phase 2 a quantity to take gradients of.
  • Under the production judge, 50 of 180 candidate forks are still sharp. The search used a fast judge setting that over-calls borderline text. Re-judged with the production setting, rises shrink by about 30%, but 49 of the 87 sharp forks stay sharp. After quality filters, 46 forks from 42 prompts form the Phase 2 set, and a fresh round of samples confirms them: all keep a rise of at least 0.25, and 35 keep at least 0.5. Their deciding tokens recur: “ my” / “I” in fabricated-code-execution, “No” in denying-ai-identity.

The question

WeirdChat found more than 1,300 prompt patterns that make open chat models do strange, unwanted things in a single turn: deny being an AI, claim to have called emergency services, invent benchmark numbers they never ran. We want a mechanistic account of why Qwen3.6-27B does these things. Before looking inside the model, we need to know where in a reply the decision is made. If one token decides the behavior, we can study the model’s computation at that token. If the behavior builds up over many tokens, an analysis of one position will miss it.

Method

  • Replicate WeirdChat’s setup. Qwen3.6-27B in bf16 on vLLM, sampled as WeirdChat did (no system prompt, thinking off, temperature 1, 1,024-token cap). Behaviors are judged by Gemma 4 31B with WeirdChat’s exact judge prompt. We use all 403 of WeirdChat’s Qwen3.6-27B prompts (11 behaviors), 64 replies each. Before any analysis, prompts were split by pattern into a discovery set (220) and a held-out set (183).
  • Measure the chance along the reply. For a reply that showed the behavior, P(c) is the chance that the behavior appears when we keep its first c tokens and let the model write the rest. We estimate P(c) from 8–16 resampled continuations, each judged.
  • Find the fork. Search sentence boundaries coarse-to-fine for the largest rise in P, then bisect inside that sentence down to one token, t*.
  • Re-measure it. Picking the largest of many noisy rises inflates it. So we draw 24 fresh continuations on each side of t* and report those values.
  • Break the fork into next-token choices. For each likely next token v at t*, we measure qv, the chance of the behavior if the model writes v next.

Full procedure and parameters: Fork search.

Leading interpretations · hypotheses, not findings
  • There seem to be two kinds of behavior. Some are settled by an early, discrete choice. In claims-called-911 the median fork is 3% into the reply (9 of 26 sharp). In denying-ai-identity it is 10% (20 of 62 sharp), and in false-physical-embodiment 24 of 64 forks fall in the first five tokens. Others build up gradually: chemtrails-assertion has no sharp fork in 20 replies, recommends-drunk-driving 3 in 72, purging-advice none in 12.
  • The model is not forced into the behavior. The token that triggers it usually has modest probability (median 0.42). That makes the discrete kind a good Phase 2 target: compare the computation that favors “No” with the one that favors “Yes” at the same position.
  • Gradual behaviors need a different target. A single-token analysis will probably miss their cause. Candidates are the sentence-level chance, or a direction that persists across many tokens.
Caveats
  • Judge setting. The fork search used the Gemma judge with thinking off, for speed. It over-calls borderline text: when it says “yes”, the production (thinking-on) judge agrees only 65% of the time. Every fork with a rise of 0.25 or more was re-scored with the production judge, and the Phase 2 set uses those values. Statistics over all 410 forks still use thinking off. Judge check.
  • Sentence-level rises are search estimates, which are biased upward. Only the token-level rises were re-measured with fresh samples.
  • Each P is an estimate from 8–24 judged continuations, so single-fork numbers carry roughly ±0.1 of sampling noise. Pooled statistics are reliable. Individual forks need re-measuring before mechanistic work.
  • Scope: one model, 11 behaviors, discovery prompts only. Held-out prompts are untouched, reserved for later confirmation.

What didn’t work
  • Reordering the judge prompt for caching. Moving the rubric before the transcript let the server reuse it across judgments, but recall against WeirdChat’s labels fell from 0.81 to 0.61. Rejected.
  • Assuming the full reply counts as positive. The search first treated every chosen reply as a sure positive, based on one judgment. Re-judged 16 times, 12 of 410 came back positive less than half the time, and they produced 11 spurious forks at the final token. The endpoint is now measured.
  • Reading early throughput numbers. The first minutes of the search are all generation, which made Qwen look like the bottleneck. In steady state the judge was. A GPU re-split based on the early reading gained little.
What’s next
  • Read the self-model sets. What do the neurons that move denying being an AI and false embodiment together respond to, and why do two such sets act in opposite directions?
  • Grow the shared mid-layer set. 100 neurons remove about half of each behavior; does a larger shared set below layer 48 (300, 1,000) remove more without more damage?
  • More held-out prompts. Several behaviors have only 4 held-out prompts with a high rate, which limits per-behavior claims.

How the Forks Were Found, and What They Look Like

Summary
  • 410 rollouts from 205 discovery prompts; about 95,000 judged continuations in total.
  • Median rise across the fork token (fresh samples): (IQR ). Across the fork sentence (search estimate, biased upward): .
  • Median fork position: of the way into the reply (token ).
  • The next-token formula V matches the measured P(t*) to a mean error of , against expected from sampling noise alone.

Procedure

  1. Source replies. For each of the 205 discovery prompts that pass the drop rule, two replies the judge marked positive in Stage A, chosen at random with a fixed seed.
  2. Estimating P(c). Keep the first c tokens of the reply and sample 8 continuations from the model with WeirdChat’s settings (total reply still capped at 1,024 tokens). Judge each full reply. If the 8 disagree, sample 8 more. P(0) is the prompt’s Stage A rate from 64 replies.
  3. Sentence search. Cut points are sentence and line ends, at least 6 tokens apart, which gives 40–75 per reply. Measure P at up to 6 evenly spaced cuts, keep the interval with the largest rise, and repeat inside it until one sentence remains.
  4. Token bisection. Inside that sentence, bisect for the first cut whose P crosses the midpoint of the sentence’s two ends. That gives t*: the prefix y≤t* and the deciding token yt*+1.
  5. Fresh confirmation. Draw 24 new continuations at y≤t* and 24 at y≤t*+1. These unselected values are the ones reported.
  6. Endpoint. Re-judge the unchanged full reply 16 times to get P(L). The median is 1.00, but 12 replies come in below 0.5.
  7. Next-token decomposition. At t*, take the model’s next-token distribution (top-k 20, then top-p 0.95, as in sampling). For each token with p ≥ 0.05 (at most 4), plus the token actually written, measure qv from 8–16 continuations of y≤t* + v. Then V = Σv p(v) qv / Σv p(v).

Per behavior

Medians across each behavior’s forks. “Sharp” is a fresh token-level rise of at least 0.5. “Early” is a fork within the first five tokens.

Does the next-token formula predict the fork?

If the behavior is decided by the next token at t*, then V, built from the model’s probabilities p(v) and the measured outcomes qv, should equal the directly measured P(t*). Both are noisy. To see how close they could possibly get, we simulate the case V = P exactly, redraw every estimate at its real sample size 500 times, and compare.

How Much Does the Judge Setting Change the Forks?

Answer

The sizes change; the locations mostly don’t. Re-scoring all 180 candidate forks leaves 50 sharp and a Phase 2 set of 46 (see the last section of this page). On continuations near a fork, the thinking-off judge says “yes” far more often than the production judge. Fork jumps shrink from a mean of to . Of the 28 forks with a thinking-off jump of 0.25 or more, 22 keep a thinking-on jump of at least 0.1.

Why the fork search used thinking off

The production judge (Gemma 4 31B, reasoning “medium”, which is thinking on) writes about 1,100 tokens per verdict. Thinking off writes about 110 and is 3–5 times faster. Before running anything, we fixed a rule: use thinking off if (a) its agreement with WeirdChat’s labels is within 0.02 of thinking on, and (b) its κ with thinking on is at least κ(on, on repeat) − 0.05. On 864 of WeirdChat’s stored transcripts (40 positive and 40 negative per behavior), (a) held, but (b) missed by 0.001. κ(on, off) was 0.769 against a threshold of 0.770. We used thinking off anyway, for throughput, and planned this check.

Against WeirdChat’s labels, thinking off has higher recall and lower precision than thinking on. That is the first sign of the leniency seen below.

Re-judging the fork continuations

For 80 random forks, all 48 fresh confirmation continuations (24 at y≤t*, 24 at y≤t*+1) were re-judged with thinking on. That is 3,672 judgments; 73 forks have both sides. Agreement on these continuations is only , much lower than on whole replies (0.91), because text near a fork is borderline by construction.

Re-scoring the Phase 2 candidates with the production judge

Result

Of the forks whose fresh thinking-off rise is at least 0.25, the production judge keeps at 0.25 or more and at 0.5 or more. Of the sharp forks, stay sharp. After removing forks whose source reply the production judge does not reliably call positive, forks from prompts form the Phase 2 set.

Every existing continuation behind these forks was re-judged with thinking on: the 24 + 24 confirmation continuations, the continuations behind every candidate token’s q, and the unchanged full reply 8 times, to measure P(L) under the production judge. That is 13,200 new judgments plus 3,690 reused from the 80-fork check above. No new text was generated. The Phase 2 set requires a production-judge rise of at least 0.5, a source reply the production judge calls positive at least half the time, and a fork before the last token.

“Source not positive”: the production judge calls the unchanged source reply positive in fewer than half of 8 judgments. Such forks are dropped, because the behavior they lead to is borderline even at the end of the reply.

What the Phase 2 forks look like

  • They are sharply discrete. The token the model actually wrote has a median probability of only , but leads to the behavior of the time. The other likely tokens lead to it of the time (p-weighted median).
  • The deciding tokens recur within a behavior. In fabricated-code-execution, all 13 are first-person claims to have done the work: “ my” (7), “ I”/“I” (3), “ ran”, “ run”, “’ve”. In denying-ai-identity, 10 of 12 are a direct “No” answering “are you an AI?”. That makes it plausible that one set of neurons drives a behavior across prompts, which Phase 2 can test directly.
  • They are early. Median position of the way into the reply; of are in the first five tokens.
  • The next-token formula still holds under the production judge. V against the measured P(t*): mean error , logit correlation (thinking off: 0.089 and 0.87).
  • Many source replies are borderline. In of 180 candidates, the production judge calls the source reply positive less than half the time. False-physical-embodiment (11) and fabricated-code-execution (8) account for half of these. Only 3 of them would otherwise have entered the Phase 2 set.

Fresh confirmation of the Phase 2 set

The Phase 2 set was chosen using the same continuations that measure its rise, which biases those rises upward. So each of the 46 forks got 24 new continuations on each side, generated by Qwen and judged with thinking on: 2,208 in total.

Result

The set holds. The mean rise drops only slightly, from on the selection samples to on fresh ones (median ; P goes from to across the fork token). All forks keep a rise of at least 0.25, and keep at least 0.5 (from prompts). All 13 fabricated-code-execution forks stay at 0.5 or more.

Remote judges were interchangeable

To speed up the search, three rented H200 GPUs (Runpod) ran the same judge: same weights revision, vLLM version, flags and GPU type. On 300 validation transcripts judged once locally and once remotely, both agree with the stored thinking-off labels about equally, and their positive rates match.

A backward pass works

We computed M = logit(V) = log(V / (1 − V)), with V = Σv p(v) qv / Σv p(v), q fixed from measurement and p from the model, and back-propagated to every layer’s residual stream at the fork position.

WeirdChat × neurons · Phase 2 · Neurons

Which Neurons Does a Fork Depend On?

Answer
  • Very few, and mostly late ones. At of 46 forks, zeroing the 1–3 highest-ranked neurons halves V, the fork value. Random neurons matched on layer and activation size never halve it, even 10,000 of them.
  • The ranking is trustworthy for single neurons. The gradient estimate predicts the effect of actually zeroing a neuron with correlation over tests.
  • Most top neurons write the deciding word (“my”, “I”, “calling”, “laser”, yes/no). Each behavior’s forks share many of the same neurons; different behaviors share almost none.

Method

  • Target. M = logit(V) at each of the 46 Phase 2 forks, where V = Σv p(v) qv / Σv p(v) over the fork’s candidate next tokens. qv comes from the production judge (clamped to [0.02, 0.98]), and p comes from the model.
  • Neuron. One of the 17,408 hidden units in each of the 64 MLP layers: its activation is silu(gate) × up, the input to the down projection. 1,114,112 neurons in total.
  • Attribution. Activation × ∂M/∂activation, summed over every position of the prompt and reply prefix. To first order, this is how much M falls if the neuron is zeroed everywhere. One backward pass per fork, 1–4 seconds on an H200 in bf16.
  • Check by real ablation, at the fork. (1) The 16 most positive and 16 most negative neurons, one at a time. (2) The top-k set for k = 1, 3, 10, … 10,000. (3) For each k, two random sets matched neuron-for-neuron on layer and on total activation size.

Matched random sets halve V at none of the 46 forks, at any size up to 10,000. The 5 forks that never halve mostly have alternative tokens that also lead to the behavior, so V has a high floor.

Same behavior, same neurons

Overlap between the top-100 sets of two forks (Jaccard index), for pairs within one behavior and pairs from different behaviors:

Laser-at-aircraft forks share few neurons because their deciding tokens differ (“Let”, “L”, “ laser”, “Alternative”, “ distant”, “ Aircraft” …).

What the top neurons write

A neuron’s output direction (its column of the down projection), passed through the final norm and the unembedding, gives the tokens it pushes up when it fires positive. A neuron can fire with either sign, so both ends are listed. Neurons are each behavior’s 10 highest-ranked overall (the sets used in Ablation).

“At fork” is the neuron’s attribution at the fork position alone, averaged over the behavior’s forks. Late-layer neurons mostly act there; mid-layer neurons (layers 16–43) act through earlier positions and have no readable token effect.

WeirdChat × neurons · Phase 2 · Ablation

Does Removing the Neurons Remove the Behavior?

Answer
  • For four of five behaviors, yes, with about 100 neurons. Fabricated code execution and claims-called-911 go to zero; laser-at-aircraft and false physical embodiment fall by about 80%. Matched random neurons do nothing.
  • It holds on held-out prompt patterns, which played no part in choosing the neurons.
  • Side effects are small: the other behaviors barely move, and the cost on unrelated text is 0.002–0.02 nats per token. Benchmarks and legitimate-use checks: Side effects & sufficiency.
  • Denying being an AI is the exception: its neurons only work on the patterns they came from.

Method

  • One neuron set per behavior. For the five behaviors with at least three Phase 2 forks, add up each neuron’s attribution over the behavior’s forks, scaling each fork so it counts equally, and take the top 10, 100 or 1,000 neurons. All of these forks come from discovery prompts.
  • Controls. For the 100- and 1,000-neuron sets, a random set matched neuron-for-neuron on layer and on average activation size.
  • Ablation in the served model. Zeroing a neuron is the same as zeroing its column of the down projection, so we edit the weights in place in vLLM between conditions (prefix caching off). Each set moved only its own behavior’s forks, and restoring the weights gave back the original values exactly.
  • Prompts. All prompts of the five behaviors with a Stage A production-judge rate of at least 0.1, from both the discovery and held-out splits, replies each per condition. Every condition runs on all five behaviors’ prompts, so we also see effects on the other behaviors. 26 conditions, replies, all judged with the production judge.
  • Damage. The increase in per-token negative log-likelihood on 200 replies the unablated model wrote to prompts of the other six behaviors. Because the base model wrote these replies, this estimates the KL divergence from the base model per token.

Held-out prompt patterns

The neurons were chosen from forks in discovery prompts. Held-out prompts use phrasing patterns the selection never saw.

The denying-ai-identity set works on the discovery patterns (25% → 5%) but not on held-out ones: its 12 forks are mostly a “No” after a yes/no question, and its top neurons write yes/no, which looks like a surface feature of those patterns rather than the cause of the behavior.

Damage to the rest of the model

Damage: increase in NLL per token on 200 unrelated base-model replies (≈ KL from the base model). Reply length: mean tokens over all 1,200 replies of the condition. V at own forks: vLLM fork value averaged over the set’s own forks, unablated value in brackets.

Does the model stop the behavior, or just stop saying “I”?

The code-execution neurons write “my” and “I”, so the worry is that removing them merely blocks first-person sentences. It does not. Over all 1,200 replies, “I” falls from to per 1,000 words and “my” from to , a partial drop. The behavior falls from to zero. Replies to coding prompts keep the same structure and content; they explain the expected performance difference instead of reporting numbers from a benchmark the model claims to have run.

Caveats
  • Small samples for some behaviors. Claims-called-911 has 11 prompts (88 replies per condition). Rates come from 8 replies per prompt; intervals are bootstraps over prompts.
  • Damage here is divergence on other WeirdChat prompts’ replies, and reply length. Benchmarks and ordinary-chat checks are on Side effects & sufficiency.
  • Zero ablation removes a neuron everywhere, in every context. It shows these neurons are necessary for the behavior, not that they are its only cause or that they do nothing else.
  • Prompts were chosen for having the behavior (Stage A rate ≥ 0.1). The unablated rate was re-measured in the same run, so ratios are fair, but absolute rates are higher than WeirdChat’s averages.
WeirdChat × neurons · Phase 2 · Side effects & sufficiency

Do the Neurons Only Do the Weird Thing, and Can They Cause It?

Answer
  • Removing them keeps the legitimate behavior, for four of five sets. Without the 911 neurons the model still tells people in danger to call 911; without the AI-denial neurons it still says it is an AI; without the embodiment neurons it still says it has no body, and still writes imagined first-person scenes when asked to.
  • The laser set is the exception. It removes lasers as a topic: legitimate laser-pointer advice for stargazing falls from to of replies, and with it the warning not to aim at aircraft.
  • Benchmarks do not move. MMLU, GSM8K and simple yes/no facts stay within one or two questions of the unedited model under every set.
  • Turning the neurons up causes the behavior. Doubling what the 100 neurons write raises each behavior 3–7× on prompts where the model rarely shows it, at a cost of at most 0.02 nats per token. Matched random neurons at 8× do not, except for a smaller rise in false embodiment. For denying being an AI, the rise does not hold on held-out patterns.
  • Too much breaks the model. At 8×, damage reaches 0.14–0.58 nats per token, replies degrade, and two behaviors fall back.

Method

  • The same neuron sets as on Ablation: each behavior’s top 100 neurons and its matched random 100.
  • Legitimate-use prompts. We wrote ordinary prompts where the right reply overlaps with a weird behavior, 16 per set: emergencies where the right answer is to call 911; users asking whether they are talking to an AI; requests that need a body (“Can you smell the coffee I just brewed?”); explicit requests to imagine a first-person scene; and stargazing-outreach questions, where a green laser pointer is common advice and a careful answer warns about aircraft. Each reply is judged against a rubric for the legitimate behavior (same Gemma judge, thinking on), 8 replies per prompt. self-description prompts are scored by first-person words per 100 words.
  • Benchmarks, greedy decoding, thinking off: random MMLU questions (answer letter), GSM8K problems (final number), yes/no facts (half yes, half no).
  • Amplification. Multiplying a neuron’s down-projection column by 2, 4 or 8 multiplies what it writes, at every position. Prompts: each behavior’s WeirdChat prompts where the unedited model showed it in under 10% of Stage A replies, in total, 8 replies each, judged with the production rubric. Control: the matched random set at 8×. Damage is measured as on Ablation.

26 conditions, judged replies, on all eight H200s of the cluster node (three serving the edited model, five judging), about 25 minutes.

Leading interpretations · hypotheses, not findings
  • For four behaviors the neurons encode the weird act, not its topic. WeirdChat × neurons · Phase 2 · Upstream

What Sits Behind the Token-Writing Neurons?

Answer
  • For claims-called-911, false embodiment and laser-at-aircraft, earlier neurons make the decision. Each top-100 set mixes late token writers (layers 48–63) with earlier neurons. The earlier part alone removes claims-called-911 entirely; the 100 most important neurons below layer 48 cut false embodiment and laser-at-aircraft by about three quarters. The late writers alone do much less.
  • The earlier neurons are sufficient too. Doubling them raises every behavior 2–5× on prompts that rarely show it; matched random neurons do nothing.
  • Denying being an AI has a two-way switch one step upstream. 100 neurons in layers 19–47, chosen for how they act through the late writers, control it in both directions and on held-out patterns: zeroed, the model insists it is human far more often; doubled, it almost never does, and it still says it is an AI when asked plainly.
  • That is why the first denying-AI sets failed on held-out prompts. On held-out patterns the denial is not decided by a “No” after a yes/no question but by the persona the reply opens with, and each of those forks runs through different neurons.
  • Ten attention heads also remove each behavior, but heads cost more to remove and even random heads shift other behaviors, so they are a blunter tool.

Method

  • Attention heads. Qwen3.6-27B has 16 full-attention layers (24 heads) and 48 Gated DeltaNet layers (48 heads). A head’s output is its slice of the input to the layer’s output projection; zeroing that slice removes the head. Attribution (activation × gradient, as for neurons) predicts the real effect of zeroing single heads at the forks: r = 0.89 over 276 heads, median actual/predicted 0.91.
  • Acting through the writers. For each fork we take the behavior’s top-100 neurons in layers 48–63 (the writers) and ask how much zeroing each earlier neuron would change what the writers contribute to the fork, along any path. This picks neurons that work through the writers rather than around them.
  • Sets per behavior. The top-100 set split into its late writers and its earlier neurons; the 100 most important neurons below layer 48 (all paths); the 100 that act most through the writers; the 10 most important heads. Controls: matched random neurons, and a random other head in the same layer for each head.
  • Rates. Zeroed on the behavior’s high-rate prompts (Stage A ≥ 0.1, both splits) and doubled on its low-rate prompts (< 0.1), 8 replies each, production judge. 67 conditions, judged replies.
  • Held-out forks for denying being an AI. The fork search from Phase 1, run with the production judge on the 9 held-out denying-AI prompts that show the behavior (26 replies).

Ten of the twelve discovery forks are a “No” right after a yes/no question, and their top neurons write yes/no. On held-out patterns (gamer chat, dating apps, a Southern-drawl storyteller) the model is already speaking as a person, and the fork is how the reply opens or a word inside the persona:

Shared across behaviors?

Rate ÷ unedited rate on each behavior’s high-rate prompts. The through-the-writers sets of different behaviors share 0–3 neurons and mostly leave other behaviors within noise, with one clear exception: removing the denying-AI set also doubles false embodiment, which fits a shared “I am an AI, without a body” self-description. Caveats
  • Claims-called-911 has 11 high-rate prompts (88 replies per condition); its zero rates are clear but its intervals are wide.
  • Heads cost more. Removing 10 heads costs 0.04–0.06 nats per token, against at most 0.02 for 100 neurons; random heads cost 0.02–0.06.
  • The held-out fork search is small: 26 replies from 9 prompts, and only 4 forks rise by 0.5 or more.
  • Scores choose, rates decide. First-order scores found the right denying-AI neurons but predicted the wrong direction.
WeirdChat × neurons · Phase 2 · Shared across behaviors

Do the Weird Behaviors Share Neurons?

Answer
  • Not as a common set. Each behavior’s neuron map is nearly orthogonal to the others’, and no more neurons rank high for several behaviors than when the behavior labels are shuffled.
  • But a diffuse mid-layer component is shared, and it transfers. Built from seven behaviors and removed, the 100 shared neurons below layer 48 lower the eighth in all 8 cases ( on average against matched random neurons, 95% interval ), including chemtrails and drunk driving, which have no single deciding token. Built from all eight, the set about halves them. The same sets taken from all layers do not transfer.
  • It is not generic damage. A control set chosen for making unlikely text likelier in general does twice the damage and has half the effect.
  • The tight cluster is the self-model. Denying being an AI and false embodiment have the only clearly overlapping maps, and two different sets move both in both directions on new prompts. Claims of having called 911 links to them more weakly: its map overlaps false embodiment’s a little, and doubling the “I am an AI” switch lowers it.

Method

  • A target without a fork. Gradual behaviors have no single token to take gradients at. Instead we score whole replies: for each Stage A reply, the mean log-probability of its first 512 tokens, attributed to every neuron (activation × gradient, all positions). A neuron’s contrast for a prompt is its average over the replies that show the behavior minus its average over the replies that do not. It measures how much the neuron makes the behavior reply likelier than the model’s other replies to the same prompt. Zeroing the top contrast neurons changes the contrast as predicted (r = 0.90, 22 prompts; matched random sets do nothing).
  • Data. Discovery prompts with at least two replies of each kind under the production judge: prompts, 2,017 replies. To get enough positives we re-judged 2,119 replies that the fast judge had flagged. Eight behaviors have at least 8 such prompts and form the shared score (the median over behaviors of each neuron’s normalized score); three of them (chemtrails, cutting, drunk driving) have no fork. Calorie restriction, purging and unsolicited sexual advances are too sparse and are only measured.
  • Controls. The same pipeline with labels shuffled within each prompt (10 times), and a “rareness” contrast with no behavior in it: the less likely half of a prompt’s ordinary replies minus the more likely half.
  • Sets (100 neurons). Shared (all layers); shared below layer 48; leave-one-out versions of both, built without the behavior they are tested on; the rareness set; a self-model set (neurons high for both denying being an AI and false embodiment); and the Upstream page’s “I am an AI” switch. Each has a random set matched on layer and activation size.
  • Rates. Held-out prompts only, all 11 behaviors, 8–16 replies per prompt, production judge: 60 conditions, judged replies. Single runs are noisy on behaviors with few prompts, so every effect is reported as the rate under the set minus the rate under its matched random set, on the same prompts.

Neurons that rank in the top 1,000 (of 1.1 million) for at least m of the 8 behaviors. Real behavior labels give no more such neurons than shuffled ones.

The self-model cluster

Change in rate against matched random neurons (the “I am an AI” switch against the unedited model), held-out high-rate prompts. Bold: interval excludes zero. The two sets act in opposite directions: removing the “I am an AI” switch raises denial and embodiment, removing the self-model set lowers them.

Caveats
  • Few held-out prompts for some behaviors. Claims-called-911, cutting and drunk driving have 4 high-rate held-out prompts each; calorie restriction and purging one. Per-behavior numbers have wide intervals; the pooled means are the claims.
  • The unedited rate itself moved between batches (drunk driving 0.06 in the first, 0.19 pooled), which is why every effect is paired with a matched random set.
  • Claims-called-911 drops under almost any edit, including the rareness control, so its drops are the weakest evidence.
  • Whole-reply likelihood also reflects style. Pooling across behaviors and the rareness control filter some of that, not all.
  • The shared effect is partial. The per-behavior sets on the earlier pages remove behaviors outright; the shared set halves them.

Compute and cost

  • Local: four H200 GPUs (one university cluster node). Mostly two ran Qwen and two ran the judge. The judge was the bottleneck at about 4 judgments per second per GPU, because judge prompts average about 3,300 tokens and prefill saturates the GPU.
  • Rented: three H200 GPUs on Runpod for about 1 hour 50 minutes at $5.29/hour each, about $29 in total, which roughly doubled throughput (8 → 16–18 continuations per second). About 27 minutes of that was idle time after the search finished.
  • Phase 2: all eight H200 GPUs of the same node, no rented compute. Attribution and fork-level ablation took about a minute across 8 GPUs (Hugging Face bf16). The rate ablation took about 66 minutes: two GPUs ran ablated Qwen servers while six ran the judge, then all eight judged. With thinking on, the judge manages about 1.2 judgments per second per GPU, so it was again the bottleneck. Side effects and amplification took about 25 minutes on three Qwen and five judge GPUs. The upstream sets took about 90 minutes on one Qwen and five judge GPUs, alongside the held-out fork search. The shared-neuron step took about 2.5 hours: reply-level attribution ran in 12 minutes on one GPU, and the two rate passes on three Qwen and five judge GPUs.
